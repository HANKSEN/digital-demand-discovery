# Questioning Patterns

Ask one question at a time. Choose the next question based on the previous answer.

## Pacing

- If the user's answer is specific and decision-ready, ask fewer follow-ups.
- If the answer contains ambiguous terms, clarify before moving on.
- After 3-5 discovery rounds, give a brief checkpoint: confirmed facts, remaining unknowns, and why the next question matters.
- After 8 discovery rounds, offer a demand snapshot before continuing.
- Stop questioning when another answer would not change the recommended solution carrier, MVP boundary, or main risk profile.
- Continue questioning when uncertainty affects the solution carrier, target outcome, data flow, or cost/resource feasibility.

## Opening Questions

- What triggered this need now?
- Who feels the pain most directly?
- What happens today when this problem appears?
- What workaround do people use now?
- What would count as a meaningful improvement?

## Socratic Questions

- What evidence shows this is the real problem?
- What assumption must be true for this solution to work?
- What would make this request unnecessary?
- What alternative explanation could exist?
- If the proposed solution failed, why would it fail?

## 5 Whys Pattern

Use when symptoms are clear but root cause is not.

```text
Problem: [symptom]
Why 1: [immediate reason]
Why 2: [underlying process reason]
Why 3: [data/role/decision reason]
Why 4: [systemic constraint]
Why 5: [root cause candidate]
```

Stop before the chain becomes speculative. Mark uncertain steps as assumptions.

## JTBD Questions

- When this situation happens, what is the user trying to get done?
- What progress are they seeking?
- What makes the current way frustrating or risky?
- What result would make them say the solution worked?

## Data and Workflow Questions

- What data enters the process?
- Who creates, reviews, updates, and consumes it?
- What states does the work item move through?
- Where does information get lost, duplicated, delayed, or distorted?
- Which decisions depend on this information?

## Minimum Coverage Checklist

Before moving to problem reframing, ensure these dimensions have been considered. They do not all need final answers; unresolved items can become assumptions or open questions.

- Target outcome: what action, decision, or state should improve?
- Current workaround: how is the job handled today?
- Breakpoints: where does the current flow fail, slow down, duplicate effort, or lose information?
- Scope in/out: what information, users, cases, channels, or domains are included or excluded?
- Volume and frequency: how much, how often, and at what time sensitivity?
- Delivery channel: where should the output or result land?
- Persistence: should outputs be stored, linked, audited, or reused?
- Constraints: budget, token/API cost, platform, language, region, access, permissions, privacy, timezone, runtime environment.
- Quality standard: what would make the user say this is good enough?
- Capability boundary: what existing tools can and cannot do.

## Scope Control Questions

- What should this not solve in the first version?
- Which feature would be useful later but unnecessary for validation?
- What manual step can remain manual for now?
- What is the smallest version that creates real value?

## Checkpoint Pattern

Use this after several rounds or when the user may feel the process is drifting:

```markdown
Current stage: [Focused Questioning / Problem Reframing / Solution Exploration]
Confirmed:
- [fact]
Still uncertain:
- [question]
Why the next question matters:
- [decision it affects]
```
